## Why it matters for you

For most of history people looked up at night and felt part of something vast and ordered. Modern physics has given that feeling a precise and surprising form: the laws of nature seem set within ranges that allow stars, chemistry and life. What that means is one of the great open questions, and you can follow the argument yourself.

## The short answer

Physicists broadly agree that if certain constants of nature were different, such as the strength of gravity, the masses of particles or the energy of empty space, stable atoms, long-lived stars and chemistry would be impossible. How narrow the ranges are is disputed: some values look extraordinarily finely set, while others could change a great deal if others changed with them. Three explanations are on the table: design, a vast multiverse in which observers can only find themselves in a life-friendly region, or a deeper law not yet known. Physics alone cannot decide between them.

## What Christians believe

Christians believe the universe is freely created by God, who is reason itself, and that its order is a gift that the mind can understand. "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1). Christians differ about fine-tuning arguments. Some see them as real evidence pointing to a designer. Others warn against a "God of the gaps", resting faith on what science has not yet explained, and prefer to see the whole of nature, not only its improbable parts, as created. Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant teaching agree that creation depends on God at every moment, not only at the start, so fine-tuning is at most a pointer, never the foundation of faith.

## The great teachers

**Augustine** (5th century) taught that God made the world with time, not in time: there was no "before" the universe (*Confessions* XI; *City of God* XI.6).

**Thomas Aquinas** (13th century) argued that the way natural things act for ends points to an intelligence that directs them, the fifth of his "five ways" (*Summa Theologiae* I, q.2, a.3). He also held that even a universe with no beginning would still depend on God for its existence.

**Johannes Kepler** (17th century) saw his laws of planetary motion as uncovering the geometry of the Creator (*Harmonices Mundi*, 1619). **Isaac Newton** (17th–18th century) wrote that "this most beautiful system of the sun, planets, and comets, could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful Being" (*Principia*, General Scholium, 1713).

## The modern debate
tag: Strongest objections included

**Georges Lemaître**, a Catholic priest and physicist, first proposed in 1931 that the universe began from a single, highly concentrated state, the seed of Big Bang cosmology; he kept his physics and theology carefully apart. In 2003 **Arvind Borde**, **Alan Guth** and **Alexander Vilenkin** proved that an expanding universe cannot be traced back forever under their assumptions, though this does not prove an absolute beginning. **Luke Barnes** concluded in 2012 that fine-tuning is a real feature of physics.

The strongest objection is the multiverse. **Brandon Carter** named the anthropic principle in 1974: we can only observe a universe that allows observers. **Steven Weinberg** used this reasoning in 1987 to anticipate a small cosmological constant, later measured in 1998. **Fred Adams** argued in 2019 that many constants could vary widely and still allow stars, and that our universe is not the most life-friendly possible. Others add that there is no agreed way to calculate how likely a constant is. Defenders of design reply that a multiverse needs finely set laws of its own, and that no other universe has been observed.
