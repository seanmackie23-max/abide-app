## Why it matters for you

You probably assume that you matter as a person, not just as someone's son or daughter, citizen or employee. That assumption has a history. Knowing where it came from shows you how precious and how fragile it is, and why the question of what grounds it still matters.

## The short answer

In the ancient world, people were defined by family, city and status: a slave, a woman, a citizen, a foreigner. The idea that every human being has equal moral worth simply as a person was revolutionary. Many historians trace it to early Christianity, which proclaimed that before God there is "neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female".

## What Christians believe

Each person is made in the image of God, known and loved by name, and responsible before God for their own life. The soul is not owned by the family, the state or a master. That is why Christians have insisted that every life, from the unborn to the dying, has a dignity no one can take away.

## The great teachers

**Paul** (1st century) wrote that in Christ the old divisions of race, status and sex no longer define a person's worth.

**Augustine** (4th–5th century) turned inward in his *Confessions*, often called the first true autobiography: a book about one person's will, memory and inner life before God.

In the Middle Ages, church lawyers developed the idea that individuals have rights by nature, which the historian Brian Tierney traced in *The Idea of Natural Rights* (1997).

## The modern debate
tag: Strongest objections included

The political philosopher **Larry Siedentop**, in *Inventing the Individual* (2014), argues that liberalism, with its equal rights and freedom of conscience, is Christianity's child, even if it later forgot its parent. **Tom Holland** makes a similar case in *Dominion* (2019).

Others disagree. Some point to Stoic philosophers, who also taught the unity of humankind. Many credit the Enlightenment, and thinkers like Kant, with doing the real work, sometimes against the churches. And critics on all sides warn that individualism, cut loose from community and duty, can leave people isolated and lonely.
