## Why it matters for you

Every person knows the feeling of being exposed, of hiding, of blaming someone else. This ancient story names that experience better than any psychology textbook, and it tells you where it came from. More importantly, it shows that even after the worst choice, God comes looking: "Where are you?" That question is still being asked of you, not in anger, but as an invitation home.

## The story

God makes a human from the dust of the ground and breathes life into him, then makes a woman as his companion. They live in a garden with one command: do not eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. A serpent persuades them that God is holding something back. They eat, and "their eyes were opened, and they knew that they were naked." They hide from God, blame each other, and leave the garden. (Genesis 2–3)

## What Christians believe

The Church calls this the Fall: humanity turned from trust in God to self-rule, and the relationship at the centre of life was broken. Everything since is touched by it: our relationships with God, with each other, with ourselves and with the world.

Paul links Adam to Christ: "as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22). Christians have long differed on whether to read the story literally or as a true story told in figurative form. Both readings have deep roots in the tradition.

## The great teachers

**Augustine** (4th–5th century) saw the Fall as the origin of a wound passed to all humanity: our love became disordered, turned inward instead of towards God.

**Thomas Aquinas** (13th century) described it as the loss of an original harmony in which reason was ordered to God and the passions to reason. Nature was wounded, not destroyed, and grace heals and perfects it.

Earlier still, **Irenaeus** (2nd century) pictured Adam and Eve as children, created to grow towards maturity with God. In his reading, the Fall was a stumble on that path.

## The modern readings
tag: Includes thinkers outside the tradition

Many have read the story as the dawn of self-consciousness. The moment "their eyes were opened" is the moment a creature first sees itself, and with it come shame, choice and the knowledge of death.

**Søren Kierkegaard**, a Christian, argued in *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844) that innocence gives way to the "dizziness of freedom": once we can choose, we are anxious. **Hegel** read the Fall as a necessary step in the growth of spirit.

**Carl Jung**, writing as a psychologist rather than a believer, saw the story as an image of consciousness separating from the unconscious wholeness of the garden. For him the path forward was not back to innocence but on to wholeness.

**Jordan Peterson**, in his 2017 lecture series on Genesis, read the eating of the fruit as the moment humans became aware of their own vulnerability and mortality, and of their own capacity for evil. With that awareness comes work, sacrifice and the need to plan for the future.

**Jonathan Pageau**, an Orthodox Christian, reads Genesis through the symbolic language of the Church Fathers: the garden as a sacred centre ordered around the tree of life, the serpent coming from its edges, and the Fall as a turning away from that centre. He develops this in *The Language of Creation* (2018).

## The sources

Read for yourself: Genesis 2:4–3:24 and Romans 5:12–21. In Hebrew, *adam* (human) comes from *adamah* (ground), and Eve's name, *Chavah*, sounds like the word for "living".

Then: Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* IV.38; Augustine, *City of God* books XIII–XIV; Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I–II, questions 81–85; Kierkegaard, *The Concept of Anxiety*; Jung, *Answer to Job*; Pageau, *The Language of Creation*; Peterson's *Biblical Series* lectures (2017, freely available online).

## Reading list

- **Start here:** Genesis 1–3, read in one sitting, slowly.
- **Start here:** C.S. Lewis, *Mere Christianity* (1952), Book Two, on what went wrong and how God answers it.
- **Go deeper:** Jonathan Pageau, *The Language of Creation* (2018), on reading Genesis symbolically.
- **Go deeper:** Jordan Peterson, *Biblical Series* lectures on Genesis (2017, free online).
- **For the scholar:** Augustine, *City of God*, books XIII–XIV; Kierkegaard, *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844).
