## Why it matters for you

You may have heard that churchgoers live longer. It is one of the most repeated claims about religion, and it is partly true. But the details matter more than the headline: what exactly is linked to health, how strong the evidence is, and what it cannot tell us. This is a place to think clearly, not to be sold something.

## The short answer

Some of the largest long-term health studies find that people who regularly attend religious services die at lower rates than people who never attend. In one study of almost 75,000 American nurses, women who attended more than once a week had about a third lower death rate over sixteen years. The link is with regular, shared practice, not with private belief. These studies are observational, so they cannot prove cause, and much of the benefit seems to travel through community. The largest trial of prayer as a cure found no benefit.

## What Christians believe

Christians do not believe faith is a health programme. Jesus healed the sick, but he also told his followers to take up their cross, and many faithful people have suffered and died young. What Christians do believe is that human beings are made for communion, with God and with one another. The first Christians met daily, shared meals and cared for the sick (Acts 2:42–47), and the letter to the Hebrews urges believers not to give up meeting together. If worship is good for people, Christians would call that a sign of how we are made, not the reason to come. All the major traditions agree that God is to be loved for his own sake, not used.

## The great teachers

**Augustine** (4th–5th century) distinguished between things we enjoy for their own sake and things we use to get something else, and taught that God alone is to be enjoyed. To use God for health or success, he thought, is to get life the wrong way round.

**Basil of Caesarea** (4th century) built a great complex outside his city with a hospital for the sick and shelter for the poor, one of the earliest of its kind.

**Benedict of Nursia** (6th century) told his monks to put the care of the sick before and above everything else, and to treat each sick person as Christ himself. Hospitals, hospices and nursing in the West grew largely from this tradition.

## The modern debate
tag: Strongest objections included

The modern research comes largely from epidemiologists such as **Tyler VanderWeele** at Harvard, whose team analysed decades of data from the Nurses' Health Study, and **Harold Koenig** at Duke, who has reviewed the field at length. In 2025 the **Global Flourishing Study**, surveying over 200,000 people in 22 countries, found that frequent attenders reported higher flourishing in every country, though the gap ranged from large in Hong Kong to almost nothing in India.

The objections deserve real weight. **Richard Sloan** of Columbia argued that early studies were weak, and that doctors should never prescribe religion, since implying that faith brings health can leave sick believers feeling guilty. Healthy people find it easier to attend, and personality or family may explain part of the link. **Olga Stavrova** found the association appears mainly where religion is the social norm. A large trial in which strangers prayed for heart-surgery patients found no benefit. A fair sceptic can say it is the community, not God. A fair reply is that few institutions build lasting, cross-generational community as reliably as congregations do, and that Christianity never claimed to be useful. It claims to be true.
