## Why it matters for you

Human rights feel obvious, but they rest on a belief: that every person has a worth that no government, majority or algorithm can take away. If you want that belief to survive, it helps to know what holds it up.

## The short answer

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) begins with "the inherent dignity" of every member of the human family. That idea did not fall from the sky. For many historians, it grew from the biblical teaching that every human being is made in the image of God, and from centuries of Christians arguing about what that means in practice.

## What Christians believe

"God created man in his own image" (Genesis 1:27). If every person bears God's image, then the weak, the poor, the sick and the stranger have the same worth as the powerful. Christians have often failed to live by this, but it has always stood in judgement over those failures.

## The great teachers

In a Roman world where unwanted babies were often abandoned, an early Christian text, the *Letter to Diognetus*, notes that Christians "do not expose their offspring".

Around 379, **Gregory of Nyssa** preached a sermon condemning slavery itself: how, he asked, can a human being, made in God's image, be bought and sold? It is one of the earliest known attacks on slavery as an institution.

**Thomas Aquinas** taught that a natural law, knowable by reason, binds every ruler.

## The modern debate
tag: Strongest objections included

The drafters of the 1948 Declaration came from many traditions: the Catholic philosopher **Jacques Maritain** influenced it, alongside the Lebanese Orthodox **Charles Malik**, the Confucian **P.C. Chang** and the secular French jurist **René Cassin**. They agreed on rights while disagreeing about their foundations.

Critics rightly point out that churches have sometimes opposed rights that we now take for granted. The Catholic Church only fully affirmed religious freedom at the Second Vatican Council in 1965. Secular humanists argue that human rights stand on reason and shared experience, and need no God. The question Christians put back is whether dignity can survive without a reason to believe in it.
