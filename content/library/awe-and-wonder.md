## Why it matters for you

Awe is the feeling of standing before something too big to take in: a mountain, a great piece of music, the night sky, the birth of a child. Psychologists have begun to study it seriously, and their early findings echo what the cathedral builders knew. It may be one of the most important feelings you rarely make time for.

## The short answer

Psychologists define awe as the response to something vast that our minds have to stretch to understand. Studies suggest awe makes people feel smaller in a healthy way, less focused on themselves and more generous. In one experiment, people who spent a minute gazing up at a grove of very tall trees were more helpful afterwards than people who looked at a tall building. Two recent reviews find a consistent link between awe and generosity. The research is young, the tests are brief and large replications are still missing, so the findings are promising rather than proven.

## What Christians believe

The Bible says that "the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom" (Proverbs 9:10), and "fear" there means something close to awe: reverence before what is far greater than us. When Job demands answers, God replies by showing him the vastness of creation, and Job is humbled (Job 38–42). Christians believe awe is meant to point beyond itself, to the Creator, and that worship is awe given a direction. The traditions express this differently: the Orthodox through icons and liturgy, Catholics through cathedrals and sacrament, Protestants through the majesty of God's word, but all see wonder as a doorway, not a destination.

## The great teachers

**Gregory of Nyssa** (4th century) taught that we come to know God not by grasping him but by entering ever deeper into a light so bright it seems like darkness, as Moses did on the mountain (*The Life of Moses*).

**Abbot Suger** (12th century) rebuilt the abbey church of Saint-Denis near Paris with soaring height and coloured glass, believing that beauty could lead the mind from what it sees to what it cannot see. It became the first great Gothic church.

**Thomas Aquinas** (13th century) wrote that wonder is a desire to know, and so a source of delight (*Summa Theologiae* I–II, q.32, a.8).

## The modern debate
tag: Strongest objections included

**Rudolf Otto**, a German theologian, described the holy in 1917 as a mystery that both makes us tremble and draws us in. In 2003 the psychologists **Dacher Keltner** and **Jonathan Haidt** defined awe by two features, vastness and the need to stretch our understanding, and founded a new field. Later experiments by **Melanie Rudd** and colleagues found awe made people feel they had more time, and **Paul Piff** and colleagues linked awe to a "small self" and to helping others.

The objections are fair. Most studies are small, use brief lab inductions such as short videos, and measure feelings minutes later, not lives over years. Large effect sizes in the reviews may partly reflect the tendency for positive results to be published. And awe needs no God: researchers study it as a natural human emotion, and it can be stirred by a rally or a demagogue as easily as by a cathedral. A Christian reply is that awe is real, and powerful, and so it matters greatly what we give it to.
