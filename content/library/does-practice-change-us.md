## Why it matters for you

Abide asks you to do small things every day: an intention, a pause, a look back at the evening. You deserve to know whether that works. The research is real, but more modest than the self-help industry suggests. Knowing what it does and doesn't show will help you practise for the right reasons, and keep going when the feelings don't come.

## The short answer

Habits do form, slowly. In one well-known study, a new daily behaviour took a median of about two months to feel automatic, with huge variation between people, and missing a single day did not undo progress. Gratitude exercises, reflective writing and meditation each show real but small benefits on average, which shrink when compared with other credible activities. Guided forgiveness programmes show some of the clearest effects. A famous finding that rituals reduce anxiety came from a study that has since been retracted. Practice changes us gradually, through repetition and attention, not through clever techniques.

## What Christians believe

Christianity has always been a practised faith, not only a believed one. Paul tells the Philippians to think about whatever is true and good, and then to do what they have learned (Philippians 4:8–9). Christians believe that real change is finally God's work, which they call grace, but that grace works through ordinary habits: prayer, reading Scripture, worship, confession, fasting and service. Catholic and Orthodox Christians give special weight to the liturgy and sacraments; Protestants to Scripture and personal prayer. All agree that practice cannot earn God's love, but it can form us to receive it.

## The great teachers

The desert monks of Egypt, whose teaching **John Cassian** (4th–5th century) carried to the West in his *Conferences*, taught that the heart is changed by small, repeated acts of attention, such as a short prayer said again and again through the day.

**Thomas Aquinas** (13th century) took up Aristotle's idea that virtues are habits, built by repeated acts until doing good becomes second nature.

**Ignatius of Loyola** (16th century) taught a daily examen in his *Spiritual Exercises*: a few minutes each evening to give thanks, review the day honestly and ask for help for tomorrow. Abide's evening review comes from it.

## The modern debate
tag: Strongest objections included

Psychology took up these questions after 2000. **Phillippa Lally** and colleagues tracked how habits form. **Robert Emmons** and **Michael McCullough** tested gratitude journals; **James Pennebaker** pioneered expressive writing; **Everett Worthington** developed forgiveness programmes. Reviews of meditation programmes find moderate evidence for easing anxiety, depression and pain.

Then came the replication crisis. Later meta-analyses, which pool many studies, found that gratitude and writing effects are small and shrink further against credible alternatives, which suggests that attention and expectation do much of the work. A widely cited 2016 study claiming rituals reduce anxiety was retracted in 2024, and a pre-registered test found no direct effect.

The strongest objection is that these are placebo effects dressed up as wisdom. A Christian objection runs the other way: treating prayer as a wellbeing technique misses its point, which is God, not mood. A fair reply to both is that small daily effects add up over years, and that the old practices were never designed to make us feel better, but to make us better people.
