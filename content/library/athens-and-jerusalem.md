## Why it matters for you

Almost everything you take for granted, from universities to the idea that truth can be argued for, grew where two ancient traditions met. Knowing those roots helps you see why reason and faith were long seen as partners, not enemies, and what might be lost if either is forgotten.

## The short answer

Western civilisation grew from two roots. From Athens came philosophy: the love of wisdom, the use of reason, the search for the good life and for beauty. From Jerusalem came the Hebrew Scriptures: one God who made the world, history with a meaning, and a fierce concern for justice and the poor. Christianity brought the two together, and for over a thousand years the West was built on that meeting.

## What Christians believe

Christians believe truth is one, because it all comes from God. Reason is a gift, not a threat to faith. John's Gospel even uses a Greek philosophical word, *Logos*, for Christ. So the questions of the philosophers and the revelation of Scripture are meant to light each other up. Traditions differ in how much weight they give to reason, but all the major churches have kept both.

## The great teachers

Around AD 200, **Tertullian** asked the famous question: "What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?" He feared philosophy would corrupt the faith.

Most disagreed. **Justin Martyr** (2nd century) taught that whatever the philosophers said truly belonged to Christ. **Augustine** (4th–5th century) said Christians should take the best of pagan learning, as the Israelites took gold out of Egypt. **Thomas Aquinas** (13th century) built his theology in dialogue with Aristotle, whose works had reached Europe through Arabic and Jewish scholars.

## The modern debate
tag: Strongest objections included

The philosopher **Leo Strauss**, in his 1967 lecture "Jerusalem and Athens", argued that the two roots are in permanent tension, and that this tension is the secret of the West's vitality. **Pope Benedict XVI**, in his 2006 Regensburg lecture, argued that the meeting of biblical faith and Greek reason was no accident but essential to Christianity itself.

Critics tell a different story. Writers such as Charles Freeman, in *The Closing of the Western Mind* (2002), argue that the rise of Christianity suppressed Greek rationalism and science for centuries. Many historians of science dispute this, pointing to the medieval universities and the Christian scholars who preserved and developed ancient learning. The argument continues, and both sides deserve a hearing.
