**Follows:** a first-year paper in biblical interpretation (hermeneutics), joined to the practice of *lectio divina*.

**Frame:** four ways of reading, as four rungs of a ladder: read it as it was written (the plain sense), read it as one story (the whole canon), read it as Jews and Christians have read it (the tradition), and let it read you (prayer). The hall ends with a practice you can keep for life.

**Real questions:** Where do I start reading the Bible? Do I have to take it literally? What do Christians mean by 'the Word of God'? Why do Christians disagree about what it says? What about the bits I find shocking? What is lectio divina? How do Jews read the Bible? Do I need a study Bible? How much should I read a day?

**Builds on:** the whole hall: *What is the Bible?* (how it came together, Augustine's Confessions XII on many true meanings), *The whole story* (the seven acts), the Old Testament journeys. **Read elsewhere:** Nehemiah 8 and Augustine *Confessions* XII (*What is the Bible?*); Luke 24 Emmaus (*What is the Bible?* and *The whole story*); Acts 8 (*Kings and prophets*): point back, do not re-read.

## Sessions

1. **Where to start.** *Answers:* Where do I start? Which translation? How much a day? *Finding:* The Bible is a library, not a novel: begin with a Gospel, a psalm and Genesis, and read slowly rather than much.
   *Read:* Psalm 119:97–104 and Joshua 1:8 (meditating 'day and night'; Psalm 1 is read in *Psalms and wisdom*: point back). *Compare:* Tyndale, *A Pathway into the Holy Scripture* (1525/1531), the opening.
   *Lecture:* reading plans across history (the lectionary, the Jewish weekly Torah portion, M'Cheyne's plan); translations compared (KJV, WEB, NRSV, ESV, NIV and their aims, formal and dynamic equivalence); study helps. *Debate:* reading through cover to cover, or by the Church's lectionary?
   *Learn:* `psalm-119-105`. *Look:* a reader with a book by a window. *Music:* `locus-iste`.
2. **The plain sense.** *Answers:* Do I have to take it literally? What did it mean to the first readers? *Finding:* The first rule of the whole tradition is to read what the text says in its own kind of writing: poetry as poetry, law as law, letters as letters.
   *Read:* Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine* III.10–15 (rules for figurative and literal). *Compare:* Luther, *Preface to the Old Testament* (1523), on the plain sense.
   *Lecture:* genre; 'literal' in the old sense (what the author means) versus 'literalist'; historical background; the Antiochene school against Alexandria. *Debate:* literal or literalist? Young-earth creationists, Augustine, modern critics (pointing back to *In the beginning*).
   *Learn:* new `2-timothy-3-16` only if not already used; otherwise `psalm-119-105`. *Look:* a scholar at a desk (Jerome). *Music:* `byrd-four-voices`.
3. **The four senses.** *Answers:* Why did the old writers find Christ everywhere? What are the four senses? *Finding:* Ancient and medieval readers read one text on four levels (literal, allegorical, moral, mystical), always resting on the literal.
   *Read:* Origen, *On First Principles* IV.2 (the body, soul and spirit of Scripture) (tr. Crombie, ANF). *Compare:* the medieval rhyme on the four senses (Augustine of Dacia), and Paul's allegory in Galatians 4:24, pointing back to *Abraham*.
   *Lecture:* Exodus read four ways (as Dante does in his letter to Can Grande); typology; the limits the tradition set. *Debate:* is allegory a key or a licence?
   *Learn:* `luke-24-32`. *Look:* a medieval allegorical subject (the Lamb, or the Fountain of Life). *Music:* `palestrina-marcelli`.
4. **Reading with others.** *Answers:* Why do Christians disagree about what it says? How do Jews read the Bible? What is 'tradition'? *Finding:* No one reads alone: Jews read with the rabbis, Catholics and Orthodox with the Church's tradition, Protestants *sola scriptura* but never *nuda scriptura*.
   *Read:* Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine* I.36–40 (any reading that does not build up love is wrong). *Compare:* *Pirkei Avot* 1:1 and 5:22 ('turn it and turn it, for everything is in it') (tr. Charles Taylor, 1897).
   *Lecture:* Scripture and tradition side by side (Trent, Orthodox, Reformed, Anglican, Jewish); the rule of faith; the role of scholars. *Debate:* who decides what it means?
   *Learn:* `matthew-22-37`. *Look:* the Church Fathers or a disputation. *Music:* `ubi-caritas`.
5. **The case against: a book that cannot be read innocently.** *Answers:* What about the bits I find shocking? Hasn't the Bible been used to justify terrible things? *Finding:* The Bible has been used to defend slavery and conquest; the tradition's own rules (read by love, read the whole, read the plain sense) are what exposed those readings.
   *Read:* Frederick Douglass, *Narrative* (1845), Appendix (on 'the Christianity of this land' and 'the Christianity of Christ') — point back if already read in *How we got here*; else the 1852 Fourth of July speech passage on the Bible. *Compare:* Matthew 13:1–23 (the parable of the soils: the same seed, different ground).
   *Lecture:* the objection at full strength: a text so plural it can mean anything; readers find what they bring (in the terms of modern reader-response critics and of Spinoza, pointing back); the slavery debate in America (Mark Noll's *The Civil War as a Theological Crisis*, our own words). *Debate:* is the problem the book or the readers?
   *Learn:* `micah-what-does-the-lord-require`. *Look:* a preacher or a congregation. *Music:* `were-you-there`.
6. **Let it read you: lectio divina.** *Answers:* What is lectio divina? How do I pray with the Bible? *Finding:* The monks read in four steps, reading, meditation, prayer and contemplation, so that the text becomes a conversation.
   *Read:* Guigo II, *The Ladder of Monks*, chapters 1–3. *Compare:* Benedict, *Rule*, chapter 48 (hours set for reading) (tr. Verheyen, 1949 is copyright; use Gasquet 1909 or Doyle's predecessor; confirm).
   *Lecture:* how lectio spread from the desert to Benedict to the Carthusians; Ignatius's imaginative contemplation; the Protestant 'quiet time'; Jewish study as prayer. *Debate:* is lectio a method or a gift? (Guigo; the Reformers; a modern teacher.)
   *Learn:* `psalm-119-105`. *Look:* a monk reading, or Jerome in his study. *Music:* `allegri-miserere`.
7. **The verdict, and a practice for life.** *Answers:* So how should I read it? What do I do tomorrow? *Finding:* Read it plainly, as one story, with the Church and on your knees: then it begins to read you.
   *Read:* James 1:22–25 (the mirror). *Compare:* Augustine, *Confessions* VIII.12 is in *Augustine* (Hall IV): point ahead only to the hall, not to a session; use instead Anthony of Egypt hearing Matthew 19:21 (Athanasius, *Life of Antony* 2, NPNF).
   *Lecture:* a fifteen-minute daily pattern; a year's reading plan; how the hall's journeys fit together. *Debate:* none; the hall's 'make the case' essay instead.
   *Learn:* new `james-1-22` (WEB). *Look:* Saint Anthony, or a figure reading by candlelight. *Music:* `jesu-joy`.

**Objection:** session 5, in the terms of those who say the Bible can be made to say anything and has been used to oppress.

**Traditions:** Scripture and tradition (Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, Anglican, Jewish), the canon (point back), the lectionary and who may preach.

**Voices:** Mark Noll; Ellen Davis; Robert Alter; John Barton, *A History of the Bible* (2019); Henri de Lubac on the four senses.

**Closing:** short answers; exam: 'Can a text have more than one true meaning?'; 'Is sola scriptura coherent?'; 'What is lost when the Bible is read only critically, or only devotionally?'. **Make the case** (ends Hall I): defend one idea from the Old Testament hall in 300 words and answer the strongest objection.
