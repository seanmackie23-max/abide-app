**Follows:** a first-year classics paper on Homeric epic.

**Frame:** two poems, two kinds of hero: Achilles, who chooses a short life and undying glory, and Odysseus, who chooses home. The journey follows the Iliad's wrath to its ending in mercy, then the Odyssey's long way home, and asks what a great life is: glory, home, or something neither poem quite reaches.

**Real questions:** Who was Homer? Did the Trojan War happen? What is the Iliad about? Who was Achilles? What is the Odyssey about? What did the Greeks mean by honour (*timē*) and glory (*kleos*)? Were the Greek gods good? Why is Homer still read? How is a Homeric hero different from a Christian saint?

**Builds on:** the first two halls (the reader now has Jerusalem's story); the memory pieces `leaves-of-the-forest`, `priam-and-achilles` and `argus-the-dog` are already in By heart. **Read elsewhere:** Hesiod (*In the beginning*, *Kings and prophets*).

## Sessions

1. **Sing, goddess, the wrath.** *Finding:* The Iliad opens on one man's wounded honour, and the whole war turns on it: Homer's subject is what anger costs. *Read:* Iliad I.1–52. *Compare:* the opening of the Odyssey I.1–10. *Lecture:* Homer, the oral tradition (Milman Parry, our own words), the Trojan War and Troy's archaeology (Schliemann, Korfmann), Greek honour. *Debate:* one poet or many? *Learn:* new `iliad-1-1` (Butler). *Look:* a Greek warrior or an ancient vase (NGA has few: flag). *Music:* none fitting; `tallis-fantasia`.
2. **The man who will not fight.** *Finding:* Achilles chooses a short life with undying glory over a long, quiet one, and Homer makes us feel both the grandeur and the waste. *Read:* Iliad IX.307–429. *Compare:* Iliad VI.390–502 (Hector and Andromache: the family the hero leaves). *Lecture:* *kleos*, *timē*, *aretē*; the warrior code; women and war in Homer. *Learn:* `leaves-of-the-forest`. *Look:* Hector and Andromache. *Music:* `faure-requiem`.
3. **The death of Hector.** *Finding:* At the height of his glory Achilles is at his most inhuman, dragging Hector's body: Homer does not hide what glory does. *Read:* Iliad XXII.247–366. *Compare:* Simone Weil, 'The Iliad, or the Poem of Force' (1940; our own words). *Lecture:* violence and pity in Homer; the gods' part. *Learn:* new `iliad-22-sixty` or similar short passage. *Look:* Achilles and Hector. *Music:* `mozart-requiem`.
4. **Priam in the tent.** *Finding:* The Iliad ends not with victory but with two enemies weeping together: mercy is the poem's last word. *Read:* Iliad XXIV.468–676. *Compare:* Genesis 50:15–21 (Joseph and his brothers, pointing back to *Abraham*). *Lecture:* why the ending matters; ransom and funeral; *xenia* (guest-friendship). *Learn:* `priam-and-achilles`. *Look:* Priam and Achilles. *Music:* `brahms-requiem`.
5. **The long way home.** *Finding:* Odysseus refuses an immortal goddess to go home to an ageing wife: the Odyssey's hero chooses a mortal, faithful life. *Read:* Odyssey V.1–227 (Calypso). *Compare:* Odyssey XXIII.153–240 (the bed: how Penelope knows him). *Lecture:* *mētis* (cunning), *nostos* (homecoming), Penelope's equal cleverness; the dog Argus (memory recalled). *Learn:* `argus-the-dog`. *Look:* Odysseus or Penelope. *Music:* `song-for-athene`.
6. **The case against: were the gods any good, and is glory worth it?** *Finding:* Homer's own heroes doubt the code, and Plato wanted to ban the poems for their gods; the strongest charge is that Homer's world has courage but no mercy beyond the tent. *Read:* Odyssey XI.465–540 (Achilles in the underworld: 'I would rather be a slave on earth'). *Compare:* Plato, *Republic* III.386a–388d (Jowett). *Lecture:* the objection at full strength: Xenophanes on the gods; Plato's censorship; Nietzsche's praise of the Homeric (our own words, pointing to the Sermon journey). *Debate:* Homer as Bible of the Greeks; Plato's charge; the Christian reading (Basil, point ahead to Hall IV as the hall). *Learn:* `smooth-the-descent` if Homeric; else `leaves-of-the-forest`. *Look:* a scene of the underworld. *Music:* `allegri-miserere`.
7. **Hero and saint: the verdict.** *Finding:* Homer gave the West its first picture of greatness (courage, honour, home) and its first glimpse of pity; Christianity kept the courage and turned glory upside down. *Read:* Philippians 2:5–11 (point back) with Iliad XXIV recalled; set reading: Iliad VI.144–149 (the leaves) is memory. Use Odyssey XIII.187–360 (Athena and Odysseus home in Ithaca). *Compare:* Augustine, *Confessions* I.13 (weeping for Dido, and Homer at school). *Lecture:* Homer in Western education; hero and saint side by side (a table). *Learn:* new `odyssey-1-1`. *Look:* a Greek landscape, or a classical scene by Claude. *Music:* `tallis-fantasia`.

**Objection:** session 6, Plato's charge against Homer's gods and the hollowness of glory, in Plato's and Achilles' own words.
