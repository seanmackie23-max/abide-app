**Follows:** a first-year paper on Hebrew poetry and wisdom literature (Introduction to the Study of the Bible).

**Frame:** the Bible's own songbook and notebook. The Psalms are Israel talking *to* God in every mood; the wisdom books are Israel thinking *about* life under God; and the journey follows the road from a happy answer (Proverbs) through the hard questions (Job is left to its own journey, Ecclesiastes here) to the claim that Wisdom has a face.

**Real questions:** What are the Psalms and who wrote them? Is it all right to be angry with God? Why are some psalms so violent or so dark? What is 'wisdom' in the Bible? Is Proverbs just common sense? Is Ecclesiastes saying life is meaningless? Why is a love poem in the Bible? Did Tolstoy really nearly kill himself, and why? How do Christians read the Psalms as about Jesus? Can I use the Psalms to pray?

**Builds on:** *Kings and prophets* (David, the exile, Psalm 137 and Psalm 51 already read there). **Read elsewhere:** Psalm 22 (*Bearing suffering*), Psalm 139 (*Dust that wonders*), Psalm 19 (*Is there a God?*), Psalm 104 (*Why beauty matters*), Psalm 8 (*Human dignity*, *In the beginning*), Psalms 102–103 (*How to pray*), Ecclesiastes 2, 3 and 12 (*A good life*, *Dust that wonders*, *Death and hope*), Job (*Bearing suffering*, and its own journey).

## Sessions

1. **The songbook of the Bible.** *Answers:* What are the Psalms? Who wrote them? How is Hebrew poetry made? *Finding:* The Psalms are a book of prayers for every human mood, collected over centuries and made to be said aloud, together.
   *Read:* Psalm 1 and Psalm 23. *Compare:* Psalm 13 (the shortest lament: 'How long?').
   *Lecture:* the five books of the Psalter and the doxologies; David and the other names in the headings (Asaph, the sons of Korah) and what 'of David' may mean; parallelism (Robert Lowth, 1753, our own words); how the Psalms were sung in the Temple and the synagogue and how monks pray all 150 every week. *Debate:* did David write them? (tradition; modern scholarship; the headings as later notes).
   *Learn:* `ps23`. *Look:* David with a harp, or King David. *Music:* `crimond`.
2. **Angry with God.** *Answers:* Is it all right to complain to God? Why are some psalms so dark? What do you do with the cursing psalms? *Finding:* Lament is the most common kind of psalm: the Bible teaches people to argue with God, not to pretend.
   *Read:* Psalm 88 (the psalm with no happy ending). *Compare:* Psalm 13 read again against Psalm 88's ending; Psalm 137:7–9 briefly (pointing back to *Kings and prophets*).
   *Lecture:* the shape of a lament (complaint, plea, trust); the cursing psalms and how Jews and Christians have read them (Augustine on the Psalms, Bonhoeffer's *Psalms: the Prayer Book of the Bible* in our own words; C. S. Lewis, *Reflections on the Psalms*, in our own words). *Debate:* should churches still sing the cursing psalms?
   *Learn:* `psalm-130`. *Look:* a mourner, or Jeremiah lamenting. *Music:* `allegri-miserere`.
3. **The beginning of wisdom.** *Answers:* What is 'wisdom' in the Bible? Is Proverbs just common sense? Who is Lady Wisdom? *Finding:* Biblical wisdom is practical skill in living, and it begins with 'the fear of the Lord': reverence, not terror.
   *Read:* Proverbs 1:1–9, Proverbs 3:1–12 and Proverbs 8:22–31 (Wisdom at God's side at creation). *Compare:* the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* (tr. F. Ll. Griffith, 1926), the passage Proverbs 22:17–24:22 resembles.
   *Lecture:* wisdom across the ancient Near East; Solomon and the scribes of Hezekiah (Proverbs 25:1); the 'two ways' (Psalm 1 again); does goodness pay? *Debate:* how much did Proverbs borrow from Egypt?
   *Learn:* new `proverbs-3-5` (Proverbs 3:5–6 WEB). *Look:* Solomon, or a scholar reading. *Music:* a setting of Psalm 1 or Proverbs if one exists in `music.json`; otherwise `be-thou-my-vision`.
4. **Vanity of vanities.** *Answers:* Is Ecclesiastes saying life is meaningless? Who was 'the Preacher'? What does *hevel* mean? *Finding:* Ecclesiastes faces the worst (death erases everything) and still tells you to eat your bread with joy: it is not despair but a cure for false hopes.
   *Read:* Ecclesiastes 1:1–11 and 9:1–12. *Compare:* Tolstoy, *A Confession*, chapters IV–VII (Maude): at the height of his fame he hides the rope and the gun, and quotes Ecclesiastes and the Buddha.
   *Lecture:* *hevel* (breath, vapour), the frame narrator and the epilogue (12:9–14, pointing back to *Death and hope*), how Jews read it at Sukkot. *Debate:* is Ecclesiastes a sceptic in the Bible, or a believer who has seen through everything else?
   *Learn:* `ecclesiastes-a-time-for-everything`. *Look:* a vanitas still life. *Music:* `brahms-requiem`.
5. **The case against: does God answer?** *Answers:* What if life really is meaningless? What if prayer is talking to an empty sky? *Finding:* The objection is real and the Bible itself voices it; its answer is not an argument but a relationship that holds on through silence.
   *Read:* Tolstoy, *A Confession*, chapters VIII–X (the reasoning that brings him to the edge; his return is kept for *The death of God?* in Hall VII). *Compare:* Psalm 88 again, as the Bible's own version of the objection.
   *Lecture:* the objection at full strength: Tolstoy's four ways out (ignorance, pleasure, strength, weakness); Schopenhauer's pessimism in our own words; the problem of divine silence (point ahead only to the Great Questions hall, not to a session). *Debate:* Tolstoy; the psalmist; a modern sceptic (in our own words).
   *Learn:* `psalm-22-1`. *Look:* a figure alone at night. *Music:* `gorecki-3`.
6. **Love as strong as death.** *Answers:* Why is a love poem in the Bible? How did Jews and Christians read it? Does God care about the body? *Finding:* The Song of Songs celebrates human love as good in itself, and the tradition read it also as the love between God and his people.
   *Read:* Song of Songs 2:8–17 and 8:6–7. *Compare:* Bernard of Clairvaux, *Sermons on the Song of Songs*, Sermon 1 (tr. Samuel Eales, 1895).
   *Lecture:* the poem as poem; Rabbi Akiva ('the Song is the Holy of Holies', Mishnah Yadayim 3:5); allegory from Origen to Bernard; the modern return to the literal sense. *Debate:* love poem, allegory, or both?
   *Learn:* new `song-8-6` (Song of Songs 8:6–7 WEB). *Look:* lovers in a garden, or the Annunciation in an enclosed garden. *Music:* `ubi-caritas`.
7. **Wisdom has a face, and the verdict.** *Answers:* How do Christians read the Psalms and Proverbs as about Jesus? What is the 'Word' in John 1? So how do we pray, think and live? *Finding:* The New Testament says the Wisdom that was with God at creation became a person; the Psalms became the prayer book of the Church, and they can become yours.
   *Read:* Colossians 1:15–20 (John 1:1–14 is read in *In the beginning*: point back and quote at most a line). *Compare:* Proverbs 8:22–31 read beside John 1.
   *Lecture:* Logos in Philo and the Stoics (brief); Jesus praying the Psalms (Psalm 22 and 31 on the cross); the Psalter in the daily office; how to begin praying a psalm a day. *Debate:* is reading Christ into the Old Testament fair? (Jewish and Christian views side by side.)
   *Learn:* `john-in-the-beginning-was-the-word`. *Look:* Christ as the Word, or St John writing. *Music:* `rachmaninoff-vigil`.

**Objection:** session 5: Ecclesiastes and Tolstoy's *Confession* (is life meaningless?) with Psalm 88 (does God answer?), stated in Tolstoy's own terms first.

**Traditions:** the numbering of the Psalms (Hebrew and Greek), the Psalter in Jewish, Orthodox, Catholic and Protestant worship (metrical psalms in Scotland and Geneva); the Song of Songs in Jewish and Christian reading.

**Voices:** C. S. Lewis, *Reflections on the Psalms* (1958); Bonhoeffer, *Psalms: The Prayer Book of the Bible* (1940); Walter Brueggemann on orientation, disorientation and reorientation; Robert Alter, *The Art of Biblical Poetry*.

**Closing:** short answers to the real questions in order; exam: 'Is Ecclesiastes a sceptical book?'; 'Why does the Bible include prayers of complaint?'; 'Wisdom in Proverbs and in John: continuity or reinterpretation?'.
