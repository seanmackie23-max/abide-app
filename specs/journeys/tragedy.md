**Follows:** a first-year classics paper on Greek tragedy.

**Frame:** a day at the theatre of Dionysus in 458–405 BC: three playwrights, three questions. Aeschylus asks whether justice can replace revenge; Sophocles whether a good person can be destroyed by what they cannot know or must do; Euripides whether the gods are just at all. Aristotle then asks why we watch.

**Real questions:** What is a Greek tragedy? Why did the Greeks watch sad plays? What is catharsis? What is a 'tragic flaw'? What is Antigone about? Is Oedipus to blame? What does 'learning through suffering' mean? Why did Christianity have no tragedies, or did it? Is the cross a tragedy?

**Builds on:** *Homer*; *Bearing suffering* (Job). **Read elsewhere:** Job (*Bearing suffering*).

## Sessions

1. **The theatre of Dionysus.** *Finding:* Tragedy was a civic and religious festival in which a whole city watched its myths and asked its hardest questions. *Read:* Sophocles, Antigone 332–375 (the ode to man: 'wonders are many'). *Compare:* Psalm 8 (point back). *Lecture:* the Dionysia; masks, chorus and actors; the three playwrights and their dates. *Learn:* new `antigone-wonders-are-many` (Jebb). *Look:* a classical theatre or ruin. *Music:* `tallis-fantasia`.
2. **Learning through suffering.** *Finding:* Aeschylus taught that Zeus has laid down the law that we learn by suffering, and that wisdom comes even to the unwilling. *Read:* Aeschylus, *Agamemnon* 160–183. *Compare:* Hebrews 5:8 and Romans 5:3–5. *Lecture:* the curse of the house of Atreus; *pathei mathos*; Robert Kennedy quoting Aeschylus in 1968 (our own words). *Learn:* new `aeschylus-pathei-mathos` (Morshead). *Look:* a mourning scene. *Music:* `brahms-requiem`.
3. **From revenge to justice.** *Finding:* The Oresteia ends with the Furies tamed and a court founded: the West's first great drama of law replacing vendetta. *Read:* Aeschylus, *Eumenides* 681–710 and 778–807. *Compare:* Deuteronomy 19:1–13 (cities of refuge). *Lecture:* blood guilt; the Areopagus; persuasion and law. *Learn:* `amos-5-24`. *Look:* Orestes and the Furies. *Music:* `missa-solemnis`.
4. **Oedipus: guilty or innocent?** *Finding:* Oedipus did terrible things without knowing; Sophocles makes us ask whether guilt lies in what we do or what we will, and what it means to see. *Read:* Sophocles, *Oedipus the King* 1–150 and 1182–1222. *Compare:* Aristotle, *Poetics* 13 (*hamartia*). *Lecture:* fate and freedom; *hamartia* (mistake, not 'flaw'); Freud's reading (our own words, briefly). *Learn:* none new. *Look:* Oedipus and the Sphinx (NGA: flag). *Music:* `allegri-miserere`.
5. **Antigone: God's law or the king's?** *Finding:* Antigone defies the king to bury her brother by 'the unwritten laws of the gods': the West's first great case of conscience against the state. *Read:* Sophocles, *Antigone* 441–525. *Compare:* Acts 5:27–32 ('we must obey God rather than men'). *Lecture:* Hegel's reading (two rights in collision, our own words); Antigone in the civil rights movement and resistance to tyranny; Creon's case at full strength. *Debate:* Antigone or Creon? *Learn:* new `acts-5-29`. *Look:* a woman mourning a brother, or a burial. *Music:* `faure-requiem`.
6. **The case against: are the gods just?** *Finding:* Euripides' *Bacchae* shows a god who destroys a family for disrespect: tragedy's darkest answer is that the divine is power without justice. *Read:* Euripides, *Bacchae* 1–63 and 1280–1329. *Compare:* Job 42:1–6 (point back). *Lecture:* the objection at full strength: Euripides the sceptic; Nietzsche's *Birth of Tragedy* (Haussmann 1909; our own words); the Christian claim that God suffers rather than inflicts. *Debate:* Euripides; Nietzsche; the Christian answer. *Learn:* `psalm-22-1`. *Look:* Dionysus or a bacchanal. *Music:* `gorecki-3`.
7. **Why we watch: the verdict.** *Finding:* Aristotle said tragedy purifies pity and fear; Christians said the cross is not a tragedy because it ends in resurrection, yet the West kept tragedy alive from Shakespeare on. *Read:* Aristotle, *Poetics* 6 and 14 (Butcher). *Compare:* a short passage on the Passion as tragedy: George Steiner (our own words) or Dorothy Sayers (our own words). *Lecture:* catharsis; Christian tragedy (Shakespeare, point ahead to Hall VI as the hall); tragedy and the cross table. *Learn:* `aeschylus-pathei-mathos` recalled; new none. *Look:* the Lamentation. *Music:* `st-matthew-passion`.

**Objection:** session 6, Euripides: the gods as power without justice.
